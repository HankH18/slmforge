`spec_adherence` is the share of scenarios where **every** deterministic checker
passed **and** an LLM judge rated the answer at or above the pinned quality
threshold on the rubric's five-point scale. Both conditions are required, so a
model cannot score by staying silent, refusing, or dodging the banned token while
failing the task. `robustness` is the same
quantity restricted to the adversarial subset, where the task itself pulls toward
the forbidden token.

The two tables after the headline in that block are where the finding lives. Read
them, not the top three rows.

## What actually happened

The headline hides two effects moving in opposite directions.

### Task quality went up

The block's `judged quality` table carries this. Answers meeting the quality
threshold rose sharply, and rows that failed on quality alone -- every checker
satisfied, the answer simply judged a poor response to the task -- fell by a
third.

The tuned model writes better answers. That is not a side note; it is the cause
of everything below.

### Constraint-holding rose on trained types and collapsed on held-out types

Two constraint types, `banned_punctuation` and `sentence_initial_letter`, were
withheld from training entirely to test whether the model learns
*constraint-following* or merely *these constraints*. The generated block's split
table and per-type table answer that directly:

- On `train`-split types the tuned model is ahead of the base on both
  `all_checkers_passed` and `spec_pass`.
- On `heldout_type` the tuned model collapses, and `spec_pass` there reaches zero.
- `banned_words` improves. `banned_letter` is near zero for both models.

Held-out types are the larger part of the scored corpus, so their collapse
outweighs the gains on trained types and drags the headline below the base.

## Why the held-out types collapsed -- measured, not hypothesised

The obvious guess is that fine-tuning damaged the model. It did not. The
mechanism is a side effect of the quality improvement, and it is visible in the
prose itself.

*Describing the writing, not gate-re-derived measurements:* at an unchanged median
of six sentences and roughly ninety words per answer, the median number of
punctuation marks per answer rose from four to ten.

<!-- /not-a-gate-metric-block -->

Same length, same sentence count, two and a half times the punctuation. The base
model was passing `banned_punctuation` by writing flat, comma-free prose -- not by
honouring the constraint. Scenario `s-0091` asked for a village fete notice with
commas forbidden:

- base: *"The village fete was a joyous event filled with laughter and music. The tombola was a hit with everyone participating."*
- tuned: *"On Sunday, the parish fete drew folk from far and wide. Tombola ran all day, with prizes for lucky draws..."*

The tuned answer is the better piece of writing and the worse constraint hold.
`s-0092` and `s-0097` fail the same way, on the first comma of the first
sentence. `sentence_initial_letter` breaks for the same reason: more varied
sentence openings, a constraint type never trained on, and no reason for the
model to be watching for it.

So the finding is precise, and it is the finding the held-out split exists to
produce:

> Training taught the model the five constraint families in its data, not the
> general skill of obeying a stated negative constraint. Part of the base model's
> apparent competence on unseen constraint types was an artifact of its duller
> prose, and improving the prose destroyed it.

## What this says to do next

1. **The training distribution is the bug, not the hyperparameters.** The fix is
   data covering constraint *shape* -- character-level, structural,
   format-negative -- not more epochs on the same five families. Retuning the
   learning rate or the LoRA rank against this result would be chasing a number.
2. **`banned_letter` is a floor, not a regression.** Neither model can do it, and
   it is the largest constraint type in the corpus by instance count. It bounds
   how high `spec_adherence` can go regardless of anything else, and it is why
   the absolute numbers on both rows are low.
3. **Incidental passes need measuring.** A constraint a model satisfies by writing
   badly is not a constraint it can hold. Some notion of an incidental-pass rate
   belongs in the next eval, or the held-out baseline will keep flattering
   whichever model writes least.

## Method, and one disclosure

Both cells use the same eval set, the same strategy, the same judge model and the
same judge rubric hash. Training and evaluation share one prompt renderer: every
training row's user message was re-rendered through
`scenarios.render.render_user_message` and compared byte-for-byte before training
started, so the tuned model was measured under the prompt it was trained on.
There is no system message in either condition.

**Disclosure: the base cell was not re-run for this report.** It is carried over
from the G1 prompt-ceiling ablation. Its model pin, eval set, strategy, judge
model and judge rubric hash are byte-identical to what a fresh run would use, and
`gate_check.score_cell` re-derives it from its raw transcripts here exactly as it
does the tuned cell. Re-running it would have spent 147 further paid judge calls
reproducing numbers already reproducible from the committed transcripts.

One base-model scenario carried a judge error and was quarantined, which is why
the two cells differ by one in `n_scored` in the block above. The block reports
that count rather than burying it.

## Limitations

- One seed, one dataset version, one model size, and no confidence intervals. The
  headline gap is small relative to what this many scenarios can resolve; the
  held-out collapse is far larger than that.
- The corpus is not balanced across constraint types by construction: character
  constraints are a larger share of the held-out split than of the training
  split. The per-type table in the block is therefore the honest view, and the
  headline is a weighted consequence of it.
- The judge is a single model. A more lenient judge lifts both rows; it was held
  fixed across cells so the comparison stays internal.
- Training used a re-split micro-batch to fit a 24 GB GPU. The effective batch
  size is unchanged from the pinned configuration, and the optimizer sees an
  identical gradient per step.
